When no database holds the food, the model’s own estimate is used instead. It is flagged, and its confidence is capped at 0.45. That happens to 1.6% of items.
02 · The constraint

The model call is the latency. Everything else is a rounding error.

126 lines, run live with nothing cached. This is every millisecond of it, by where it went.

It is a generation, charged by the token: about 12 ms per token written. The shortest third of parses write 284 tokens, the longest third 566. The rest of the spread is the provider’s queue.

Two levels, two keys

Neither key carries a user id, so a food somebody else logged is already warm for you — and the two levels are independent, which is how a line nobody has ever typed still gets its foods for free.
03 · The evidence

04 · The root cause

The old score could not tell ten rows apart.

It counted how many of your words a row contains. One word, ten rows, ten perfect scores. The new score reads where an unmatched word sits inside the row: before the comma it changes the food, after it, it only narrows it down.

06 · What comes next

Somebody fixing their own lunch is a free label.

Every one of the 126 test cases was written by hand. This loop produces the same thing for nothing, from the foods people actually eat. It is built end to end and it runs; what it does not have yet is users.

07 · Beyond the parse

It offers what you eat, at the hour you eat it.

Ninety days of the person’s own log, folded into half hour buckets. Nothing on this page calls a model.

The curve is normalised against the food’s own hours, so something eaten all day answers yes at every hour — which is correct, it is always plausible. And the fit is floored at 0.25, so the wrong hour never wipes a food out. It only sinks it.

What a tap costs

A suggestion is, by construction, a line this person has logged before — so it lands on the parse cache. No model call, no tokens, no wait, and the answer that comes back is the one they already accepted.
